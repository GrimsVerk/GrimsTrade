## A. What the research literature says

This report puts the literature first. This section covers three questions in turn: how to spot a late-stage bubble, how to leave one, and where to wait. Section C then shows how the playbook follows from it. Details and full citations are in `lanes/lit.md` (lit), `lanes/haven.md` (haven) and `lanes/bt.md` (bt, our backtests). "(unverified)" means the figure was read only from a summary or from memory in this run (lit §17; section 7.5). Some papers are cited from memory in lit and were not reopened: Campbell-Shiller, Goyal-Welch 2008, Baker-Wurgler 2006, Abreu-Brunnermeier, Fisher-Statman and Pan-Poteshman. They are standard results.

### A.1 Recognising a late-stage bubble

One finding runs through all of it. The indicators that predict anything do so over 1-10 years, and most lose their edge out of sample. [Goyal-Welch (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhm014) found that the classic predictors mostly fail to beat the historical average. [Goyal-Welch-Zafirov (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhae044) re-tested them with data to 2021: of the predictors still significant in sample, about half predict poorly out of sample. Over six weeks the expected gain is about 0.6-0.8% of the portfolio, against a typical swing of 5.4-6.8% (lit §4; our arithmetic). So for each indicator we ask two things: does it raise the risk, and does it give a date?

| Indicator | What the research finds | Horizon | Evidence quality | Out-of-sample record; our own test | Usable in 6 weeks? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Valuation (CAPE, dividend yield)** | A high CAPE predicts low real returns. The one-year link is small: R² about 0.09 at 1 year and 0.3 at 5 years for the dividend yield (Cochrane 2011, unverified) ([Campbell-Shiller 1998](https://doi.org/10.3905/jpm.24.2.11); lit §3) | 5-10+ years | Strong at 10 years, weak at 1 year | Mostly fails out of sample (Goyal-Welch). With sign restrictions there is a small gain ([Campbell-Thompson 2008](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhm055)). **Ours:** the CAPE exit rules avoided a third of the fall in only 1-2 of 8 crashes; they were out from June 1997 and missed the +100% that followed (bt Part B) | **No.** CAPE says "expensive" (39.4 now, 98th percentile), not "when" |
| **Industry run-up (Greenwood-Shleifer-You)** | US industries 1928-2014, after a two-year rise of 100% or 150%, raw and net of the market: P(40% fall within 2 years) was 53% and about 80%, against a 14% base rate. The odds rise further with rising volatility, share issuance, acceleration and young firms. Turnover and sales growth do not help. In the 21 run-ups that crashed, the peak came on average 6 months and +30% after the flag ([GSY 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2018.09.002); [NBER w23191](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23191); lit §1) | 2 years | Good: 40 US and 107 international episodes. The thresholds are in-sample means | International sample: 50% after 100%, 67% after 150%. **Ours** (gsy-1, 49 Ken French industries 1928-2014): 38% after a 100% run-up (104 run-ups) and 51% after 150% (51), against 15% for any industry-month. Since 2002: 27% and 35% against 12%. Our definition gives more episodes than GSY's 40, and 20 of ours are 1933-35 rebounds, so the levels differ. The direction holds: odds 2-3 times the base rate. The median next 6 months was +3% after 100% and -6% after 150%, across all run-ups, crashed or not. As an overlay on a trend rule the flag added about +0.03 Sharpe and did not cut the worst loss materially, because the trend rule was already out for 88% of industry crashes (310 of 351, 1928-2001) | **As a risk flag, not a date.** `^SOX` +99.5% over 2 years on 17 Nov, at the flag; broad US tech (`HiTec`) +62% (bt amendment) |
| **Same, whole markets (Goetzmann)** | In national markets since the early 1900s, a crash after a boom is "only slightly" more likely than otherwise. After a market doubled, there was about a 10% chance it gave back the whole gain ([Goetzmann 2015](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21693), abstract) | Years | Medium: broad panel, abstract read | n/a | The counterweight to GSY: GSY's high odds are for industries, not whole markets |
| **Issuance and IPO waves** (a commonly heard idea; details in section 6.1) | In GSY, issuance flagged 13 run-ups, of which 9 crashed, but it missed 13 other crashes. The equity share of new issues predicted market returns 1928-1997 ([Baker-Wurgler 2000](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~jwurgler/papers/equityshare.pdf); later record unverified). Firms go public after strong returns, so a wave does not prove a bubble ([Pastor-Veronesi 2005](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v60y2005i4p1713-1757.html)) | 1-2 years | Medium | **Ours:** of 11 hot IPO years since 1980, a bear market began within two years after 4. The threshold was chosen after seeing the table (section 6.1) | **No** |
| **Breadth and concentration** | Across 49 US industries 1926-2025, concentration explains "virtually none" of future returns. A rule that held less when concentration was high lowered returns and raised risk ([Kritzman-Turkington 2025](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5436695), read via a [summary](https://quantpedia.com/the-fallacy-of-concentration-risk/)). Practitioner studies find that high top-10 concentration preceded equal-weight beating cap-weight over 5 years (unverified). Advance/decline divergences, "% above the 200-day" and the Hindenburg Omen: no peer-reviewed support found (a short search; absence, not proof) (lit §5) | 5 years, and only for which stocks do better | Weak as a timer | Acting on concentration lost in their test. Not tested by us | **No.** At most a reason to buy back broad or equal-weight |
| **Sentiment indices** | When the Baker-Wurgler index is high, speculative stocks later do worse than safe ones. That is a cross-section result at about 1 year ([BW 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2006.00885.x); [BW 2007](https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.21.2.129)). A cleaned version predicts the monthly market return in and out of sample, 1965-2010 ([Huang-Jiang-Tu-Zhou 2015](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhu080); out-of-sample R² unverified). The AAII bull-bear survey is a modest, noisy contrarian signal ([Fisher-Statman 2000](https://doi.org/10.2469/faj.v56.n2.2340), unverified). For put/call, the robust result is for single stocks using non-public volume ([Pan-Poteshman 2006](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhj024)); for the index put/call ratio no solid evidence was found (lit §7) | Months to 1 year | Medium (Baker-Wurgler) to weak (AAII, put/call) | Even a few percent of monthly R² moves six-week expected returns by only a fraction of a percent (lit §4). Not tested by us | **No.** No defensible threshold |
| **Credit spreads** | The excess bond premium leads recessions ([Gilchrist-Zakrajšek 2012](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.102.4.1692)). Very tight spreads two years earlier predict widening and weaker activity ([López-Salido-Stein-Zakrajšek 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjx014)). Tight spreads are the warning; widening is the event. The literature gives no stock-sell threshold (lit §6) | 1-2 years (for the economy) | Medium for recessions; none for stock timing | **Ours:** the Baa-spread rule caught 2000 and 2007 but was late in 1987 and 2020. 87-95% of the credit rules' exits were false alarms (bt Part B) | **Only as a confirmer.** Calm now: Baa spread 1.51 |
| **Volatility, VIX** | Cutting exposure when recent variance is high raised Sharpe ratios in sample ([Moreira-Muir 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.12513)). Across 103 strategies, real-time versions mostly did not beat the unmanaged ones ([Cederburg et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2020.04.015)). The VIX curve's slope predicts returns on volatility products, not on the S&P ([Johnson 2017](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022109017000825)). An inverted curve marks stress already under way, and high VIX was often followed by above-average returns (lit §9) | Weeks to months | Medium in sample, weak out of sample | **Ours:** VIX above 30 caught 2007-09, 2020 and 2022 (data from 1990). A volatility spike alone protected poorly. The VIX trigger failed the six-week bar (bt Parts A, B, amendment) | **Only as a confirmer.** VIX 25.5 and the curve inverted now |
| **Trend and momentum** | 12-month time-series momentum works in each of 58 futures; it persists 1-12 months and then partly reverses ([Moskowitz-Ooi-Pedersen 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2011.11.003)). Trend following was positive in 8 of the 10 worst 60/40 drawdowns since 1880 ([Hurst-Ooi-Pedersen 2017](https://doi.org/10.3905/jpm.2017.44.1.015)). It fails in sudden crashes like 1987 (lit §8) | Reacts after the turn | Strong over the long run: the data before 1985 are out of sample relative to the 2012 paper | The honest out-of-sample gain is small, and most exits are whipsaws (A.2). **Ours:** the 200-day rule avoided at least a third of the fall in all 8 big crashes; 89% of its exits were false alarms (bt Part B) | **Yes.** It is the one reaction rule (A.2, A.3) |
| **LPPL (Sornette)** | Fits a "critical time" to super-exponential price growth ([Johansen-Ledoit-Sornette 2000](https://doi.org/10.1142/S0219024900000115)) | Weeks to months (claimed) | Weak | On 11 Hang Seng crashes, only 7 fits had parameters in the ranges proposed after the fact ([Bree-Joseph](https://arxiv.org/abs/1002.1010)). No public record of live hits and false alarms was found (lit §10). Not tested by us | **No.** The fits are unstable |
| **PSY / GSADF** | A real-time test of whether prices are explosive. It dates the start of the 1990s Nasdaq episode years before 2000 ([Phillips-Shi-Yu 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/iere.12132); [Phillips-Wu-Yu 2011](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2354.2010.00625.x)) | Dates the start; dates the end only after prices turn | Medium as a bubble detector | Used as an exit it behaves like a trend rule (lit §11). Not tested by us (no tool) | **No** as a top timer |

**What the table says.** Everything that warns of a bubble works over years, and nothing names a date. Trend is the only indicator with a long out-of-sample record that acts within weeks, and it acts after the turn, not before. Theory agrees. In Abreu-Brunnermeier (2003), informed traders cannot coordinate their exit, so a bubble outlives "everyone knows" and minor news can set off the crash. By construction there is no public top signal ([Econometrica](https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-0262.00393); lit §2).

### A.2 Leaving a bubble

- **Trend following.** Faber's rule: hold the index while its month-end close is above its 10-month average, otherwise hold cash. He reports similar results for 3 to 12+ month averages and notes that 10 months is close to the 200-day average ([Faber 2007](https://doi.org/10.3905/jwm.2007.674809)). His reported results for 1901-2012: 10.18% a year against 9.32% for buy-and-hold, with the 1930s fall cut from about 84% to 42% (unverified, secondary summaries). Hurst-Ooi-Pedersen covered 67 markets, 1880-2016. The 10 worst 60/40 drawdowns lasted about 15 months on average, which gives the signal time to flip. Acting one month late hurts ([AQR copy](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/A-Century-of-Evidence-on-Trend-Following-Investing); lit §8).
- **The honest record: whipsaws.** Zakamulin (2014) chose parameters out of sample and charged 0.5% costs. On the S&P 500 1930-2012, the Sharpe gain shrank to about 7-12%, and in about 80% of the spells out of the market the timer did worse than buy-and-hold (unverified, CXO summary). All of the benefit came from a few severe bear markets ([JAM](https://doi.org/10.1057/jam.2014.25)). **Ours:** over 98 years the trend rules ended level with buy-and-hold on return, with about a third less maximum drawdown. Their cost in normal years was 1.2-3.5% a year: insurance with a premium (bt Part B). For you, the whipsaw cost nearly vanishes. You must sell by year-end anyway, so a trigger only moves the date earlier (lit §8).
- **Stop-losses.** If prices are a random walk, a stop-loss always lowers expected return. It adds value only when returns have momentum or regimes switch. In the working paper, rules added 50-100 bp per stop-out month in 1950-2004, with long Treasuries as the place to wait. The exact thresholds are unverified ([Kaminski-Lo 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.finmar.2013.07.001); lit §12). **Ours:** 8% and 10% drawdown stops protected in all 8 crashes but bought back too early in long bear markets. A Kaminski-Lo-style rule did clearly worse than the trend rules. A 5% trailing stop failed the six-week bar (bt Part B, amendment, Part A).
- **Volatility management.** It helps in sample (Moreira-Muir) and is weak in real time (Cederburg et al.). **Ours:** using volatility as a confirmer for the 200-day rule cut trades but let 2000-02 through (-42%) (bt amendment).
- **Timing overall.** 237 newsletter strategies (1980-1992) showed no ability to raise stock weights before rises or cut them before falls ([Graham-Harvey 1996](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X(96)00878-1)). Investors' actual (dollar-weighted) returns trailed buy-and-hold by about 1.3% a year on NYSE/AMEX 1926-2002, and by 5.3% a year on Nasdaq 1973-2002 ([Dichev 2007](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.97.1.386); numbers unverified). The literature backs rules that cut exposure after a regime change, not forecasts of tops (lit §13).
- **Riding and scaling out.** Hedge funds rode the tech bubble and cut their positions stock by stock. Their tech exposure peaked around September 1999, about six months before the top (detail unverified) ([Brunnermeier-Nagel 2004](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2004.00690.x); lit §2).
- **Selling at once or in stages.** Lump-sum investing beat a 3-month cost-averaging plan 68% of the time (1976-2022). Staging won in the worst 5% of cases and lowered regret: "investors with higher loss aversion would be better off with CA" ([Vanguard 2023](https://corporate.vanguard.com/content/dam/corp/research/pdf/cost_averaging_invest_now_or_temporarily_hold_your_cash.pdf)). A fixed schedule is beaten by a policy that acts on new information ([Constantinides 1979](https://doi.org/10.2307/2330513)). Turned round for selling: over six weeks, staging costs a small fraction of a percent in expectation and caps regret either way (lit §14).

### A.3 The ranked shortlist, and where each threshold comes from

Ranking = strength of evidence × usability within six weeks (lit §15).

| Rank | Indicator and threshold | Threshold from | What our tests found | In the playbook? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Trend break on the holding's own index.** Month-end close below the 10-month average (Faber). Daily form: close below the 200-day average. Slower form: 12-month return below 0 (MOP) | 10-month and 12-month: **literature**. 200-day daily: **heuristic** close to Faber; no paper pins it. 2% band or 2-day confirmation: **heuristic** | 200-day: avoided at least a third of the fall in 8 of 8 crashes; held to the trough it lost 11-19%, against 25-86% for buy-and-hold. The band or 2-day versions halve the trades but were late in 1987 (-33% vs -16%). 12-month momentum missed 1987 and 2020 (bt Part B, amendment) | **Yes:** the speed-up trigger, on the first close below |
| 2 | **Drawdown stop**, -8% to -10% from the recent high | **Heuristic.** Kaminski-Lo give the condition, not the number | Protected in 8 of 8 crashes but bought back too early in long bears. A trailing stop failed the six-week bar | No |
| 3 | **Volatility spike after calm**: 21-day realized volatility above about 2x its 1-year median, or VIX above 30, together with rank 1 or 2 | **Heuristic.** Moreira-Muir give the direction | The VIX trigger failed the six-week bar; volatility alone protected poorly | No for selling. For re-entry, VIX above about 45 brings a tranche forward |
| 4 | **Credit stress**: high-yield spread or the excess bond premium rising quickly | **No literature threshold.** bt used Baa spread 0.5 above its 1-year low, and HYG/LQD below its 200-day average (heuristics) | Late in 1987 and 2020; 87-95% false alarms | No for selling. For re-entry, a spread peak can bring a tranche forward |
| 5 | **GSY flag on the AI industry**: two-year return above 100%, raw and net of the market, plus rising volatility, issuance, acceleration and young firms | **Literature** (in-sample means) | Replication: 38% and 51% against 15%. As a trend overlay it added little (gsy-1) | As background only: a reason to have a plan and to wait in cash rather than the broad market. Not a trigger |

**Not supported for a six-week decision** (lit §15): CAPE or any valuation level; concentration and breadth; LPPL; GSADF as a top timer; put/call and AAII; VIX-curve inversion; seasonality (the "Halloween" effect: [Bouman-Jacobsen 2002](https://doi.org/10.1257/000282802762024683), from memory; if anything a weak reason not to rush); newsletter and pundit calls.

**So the playbook's working trigger is not a paper's number.** The 200-day daily line is a heuristic close to Faber's 10-month rule. Our backtests tested it on 8 crashes, with the card's bar fixed before the results. Combining it with staged selling was added after the first results were seen (post hoc).

### A.4 Havens in the literature (details and our crash table: section 4)

- **Gold is a haven only briefly.** It is a haven for stocks "only for 15 trading days after an extreme shock", and neither a hedge nor a haven for bonds ([Baur-Lucey 2010, WP](https://www.tcd.ie/triss/assets/PDFs/iiis/iiisdp198.pdf)). Over 1979-2009 it was a hedge and haven for the US and major European markets, but not for Australia, Canada, Japan or the BRICs ([Baur-McDermott 2010](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jbfina:v:34:y:2010:i:8:p:1886-1898)). **Ours:** gold fell 22% in dollars inside the Lehman weeks and 12% in March 2020 (haven).
- **Haven currencies.** CHF and JPY gain against the dollar over hours to days when US stocks fall (1993-2008) ([Ranaldo-Söderlind 2010](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:revfin:v:14:y:2010:i:3:p:385-407)). **Ours:** JPY nevertheless fell 21.5% against the dollar in 2022.
- **Flight to quality, and when it fails.** In the March 2020 "dash for cash", even government bonds were sold ([FSB 2020](https://www.fsb.org/2020/11/holistic-review-of-the-march-market-turmoil/)). Stocks and bonds fall together when inflation uncertainty dominates ([Brixton et al. 2023](https://www.aqr.com/insights/research/journal-article/a-changing-stock-bond-correlation)). **Ours:** long Bunds lost 32% in 2022.
- **Money funds can break.** The Reserve Primary Fund fell to 97 cents on 16 Sep 2008 ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/litreleases/2009/lr21025.htm)). Between 11 and 25 March 2020, euro-area LVNAV funds lost about EUR 85bn (16% of assets), while public-debt funds had inflows ([ECB](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/pub/financial-stability/macroprudential-bulletin/html/ecb.mpbu202104_2~a205b46756.en.html)).
- **Trend following as "crisis alpha"** (Hurst-Ooi-Pedersen; [Neville et al. 2021](https://www.man.com/maninstitute/best-strategies-for-inflationary-times)). Both come from firms that sell trend products. It is a strategy with losing years, not a parking place.
- **Upshot:** every haven in the literature is conditional on the kind of crash. In our crash table, euro cash is the only holding that never lost value (section 4).

**Lessons** (inv, cross-investor lessons):

1. **Nobody with a documented record timed a top to the month.** Buffett was more than a year early, Grantham 2-3 years, Shiller 3+ years. Robertson quit 20 days before he would have been proved right. Those who were right say they cannot time it (Buffett, Marks, Klarman).
2. **Being early is expensive and can end you first.** Tiger closed; GMO lost about a third of its assets; Hussman's fund shrank by more than 90%. A plan must survive the pop coming late.
3. **The winners scale; they do not flip.** Buffett built cash over years, Marks turns a "risk dial", Klarman's cash is a residual, and hedge funds cut tech stock by stock (A.2).
4. **Re-entry is where the money was made**, with rules set in advance. They were early there too: Buffett's op-ed came 28.5% before the low, and Oaktree started buying in September 2008. Several who called the top missed the re-entry (Grantham, Hussman).
5. **Chasing, and valuation-only calls, hurt even the best.** Druckenmiller bought the 2000 top and sold Nvidia too early on valuation.
6. **Shocks are not forecastable**, even by the largest macro firm (Dalio 2020). Crash safety must come from the asset you hold (cash), not from a call. The one clean bubble trade, Templeton's, used a dated mechanical trigger, not general bubble talk.

**Left out on purpose:** CAPE, breadth and concentration, sentiment indices, LPPL, GSADF and IPO counts as sell triggers. The literature gives them no date (A.1, A.3), and our tests agree for CAPE, VIX and credit. VIX and credit appear only as a way to bring a re-entry tranche forward.

**Ours, not the literature's:** the choice of two tranches, the 200-day daily form, the fallback mechanics and the re-entry levels all come from our own backtests. Fixed in advance: the six-week bar, the 8-crash rule test and the re-entry card. Chosen after seeing results: the "half now plus 200-day" combination, the `now-like` sample, the 300-IPO threshold and the 10-firm filter in the GSY replication (section 7.2). Commonly heard ideas (an IPO wave as a top signal, Berkshire, a savings account, bonds) are assessed after this evidence, in section 6.
